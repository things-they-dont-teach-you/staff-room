# Build retirement courses, keep money lessons short

Build a separate "Retirement and investing" course for each country, plus an eight-lesson country-neutral principles course. Shrink money-course lessons 7 and 8 into short gateway lessons that teach one universal idea each and hand off to the country course. Each country needs six to nine lessons of its own: South Africa's two-pot system and cumulative lump-sum tax tables, the UK's auto-enrolment and post-Budget ISA rules, and US 401(k) matching and Social Security claiming ages. Those facts change on different budget cycles. Two lessons per country cannot hold them, and it would be dangerous to try. The principles course should lead with the concepts adults fail most often worldwide: **only 42% answer a compound-interest question correctly, 59% understand diversification and 63% can apply inflation to their own savings**. Fees and index-fund evidence come next, and they are the most actionable. Licensing shapes how the course can be built. The best index-fund evidence (S&P SPIVA) forbids reproduction and storage "in a database or retrieval system". FINRA bans use with machine-learning programs. FCA material may not be modified, and MoneyHelper forbids distributing modified versions. None of those four can go into an AI tutor's corpus. US federal sources and GOV.UK can be adapted freely, and South African regulator pages can only be paraphrased and linked. Several key facts changed in the last few weeks or are still pending. They include the scrapped UK cash-ISA cut (28 Oct 2026), the UK Lifetime ISA's closure to new savers in 2029, the US Saver's Match from 2027 and SA's R2m offshore allowance. Each lesson therefore needs a "facts as at" date.

## Eight universal lessons form the country-neutral spine

The global course should start where literacy is weakest and the stakes compound longest. The OECD/INFE 2023 survey of 39 countries found that **only 34% of adults reach the minimum financial-literacy target**. Compound interest (42%) and applying inflation (63%) have been weak spots in every round since 2016. **Only 37% check whether a provider is regulated, and 15% report being scammed** ([OECD/INFE 2023](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/12/oecd-infe-2023-international-survey-of-adult-financial-literacy_8ce94e2c/56003a32-en.pdf)). The Lusardi–Mitchell "Big Three" questions on interest, inflation and diversification test exactly these concepts and are country-neutral once "$" becomes "100 units" ([GFLEC](https://gflec.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/3-Questions-Article2.pdf)). Use them as the AI tutor's pre-test and post-test. Stanford hosts a free version ([IFDM quiz](https://ifdm.stanford.edu/the-big-three/quiz)).

Moving scams from eighth to third departs from the research notes' order on purpose. The harm is immediate and irreversible. The "check the register" habit is also the one action that carries over to every country course.

The fee lesson works best with a public-domain anchor plus a longer illustration. The SEC's worked example takes **$100,000 growing at 4% a year for 20 years: it ends at about $179,000 after a 1% annual fee and about $198,000 at 0.50%** ([SEC mutual fund fees](https://www.sec.gov/files/ib_mutualfundfees.pdf)). That understates a 40-year career. Run at 7% gross over 40 years, the same 100,000 grows to about 1.47 million with a 0.05% tracker fee, 1.03 million at 1% and 704,000 at 2%. **A 2% fee therefore costs roughly half the final pot.** These figures were computed for this research rather than taken from a source, so re-run them in the [Investor.gov calculator](https://www.investor.gov/free-financial-planning-tools) before publishing. The same holds for the early-bird example: 100 a month from 25 to 35 at 7% ends at about 131,800 at 65, which beats 100 a month from 35 to 65 (about 122,000) with a third of the contributions.

The index-fund lesson has unusually consistent evidence. To end-2025, **95.6% of South African equity funds underperformed the S&P South Africa 50 over 10 years**. So did **100% of rand-denominated global equity funds**, and fewer than half of SA equity funds (48.5%) even survived the decade ([SPIVA South Africa YE2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-south-africa-year-end-2025.pdf)). In the US, **85.6% of large-cap funds lagged the S&P 500 over 10 years and 92.9% over 20**, and only 34.6% survived 20 years ([SPIVA U.S. YE2025, third-party mirror](https://www.ariesadvisors.com/files/spiva-us-year-end-2025.pdf)). Teach this as long-run evidence. Short windows swing: the US 3-year figure is 66.8% against 89.0% over 5 years. And S&P's own mid-2026 report found SA short-term bond funds to be the one category where active managers mostly won ([SPIVA SA Mid-Year 2026](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-south-africa/)).

Lessons 7 and 8 need caveats built in. Morningstar puts the average US investor's shortfall at **1.2 percentage points a year over the decade to 2025: 8.7% earned against 9.9% from the funds themselves** ([Morningstar Mind the Gap 2026](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap)). A 2026 *Financial Analysts Journal* paper argues that the gap should not be blamed on bad timing ([Fulkerson et al.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0015198X.2026.2657253)). Teach "automate and stay invested" as behaviour, and present the size of the gap as debated. Use DALBAR figures not at all, since they are paywalled and their method is contested. For retirement targets, OECD mandatory systems replace on average **63% of an average earner's net pay**, ranging from under 35% in Ireland and Lithuania to 96% in the Netherlands ([OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/11/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_76510fe4/full-report/net-pension-replacement-rates_a7a9e376.html)). Morningstar's forward-looking safe withdrawal rate is **3.9%** for 30 years at 90% success, up from 3.3% in 2021 ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/whats-safe-retirement-withdrawal-rate-2026)). The 4% rule should therefore be taught as a US-derived rough guide. Fees reduce it, and retiring into a crash hurts it most. That second point is sequence risk, and no regulator source explains it with a worked example, so the lesson should use computed numbers.

## South Africa's course turns on one rule: don't cash out

The SA course has the highest stakes and the most local complexity. The two-pot system, live since **1 September 2024**, splits every pension, provident and retirement-annuity (RA) fund into three pots. The vested pot holds pre-2024 money under the old rules. The savings pot takes one-third of new contributions and allows one withdrawal per tax year. The retirement pot takes two-thirds and is locked until it buys an annuity ([FSCA myMoney](https://www.fscamymoney.co.za/Pages/two-pot-system-retirement-system-FAQ.aspx)). The behaviour data shows why the first lesson must be "preserve". By 31 January 2025, SARS had approved **2.4 million tax directives paying out R43.4bn**, and it flagged 213,654 applicants for under-declaring income ([SARS](https://www.sars.gov.za/media-release/more-than-2-million-taxpayers-withdraw-from-their-savings-pot/)). In March 2026, two-thirds of Alexforbes members who had claimed in the previous year claimed again ([Citizen](https://www.citizen.co.za/business/you-can-draw-from-your-two-pot-savings-again-but-you-probably-shouldnt)).

The tax tables make the cost concrete. Both lump-sum tables are **cumulative over a lifetime**, and SARS says neither changed for 2026 or 2027 ([SARS lump-sum tables](https://www.sars.gov.za/tax-rates/income-tax/retirement-lump-sum-benefits/)). The withdrawal table, which applies to cashing out a vested pot on resignation, has only **R27,500 tax-free**, then charges 18% up to R726,000. The retirement table has **R550,000 tax-free**. Applying those tables, cashing out a R100,000 vested pot on a first job change costs **R13,050 in tax**. It also uses up R100,000 of the tax-free amount that would otherwise be available at retirement. By contrast, a first R550,000 retirement lump sum is tax-free. Savings-pot withdrawals are taxed differently again, at the marginal rate of 18–45%, after fund fees and any SARS debt are deducted. Small pots get a reprieve under the **R165,000 de minimis rule**: if two-thirds of the vested pot plus the retirement pot comes to R165,000 or less, the member may take it all in cash ([FSCA](https://www.fscamymoney.co.za/Pages/two-pot-system-retirement-system-FAQ.aspx)).

Drop the much-repeated claim that "only 6% can retire comfortably". No primary Treasury source for it was found.

## The UK course starts with the employer's free 3%

The UK system is built around auto-enrolment. The first lesson is therefore simple: don't opt out. Employers must enrol workers aged 22 to State Pension age who earn over **£10,000**. The minimum contribution is **8% of qualifying earnings between £6,240 and £50,270, at least 3% of it from the employer**. The thresholds are unchanged for 2026/27 ([TPR](https://www.thepensionsregulator.gov.uk/en/employers/new-employers/im-an-employer-who-has-to-provide-a-pension/declare-your-compliance/ongoing-duties-for-employers/earnings-thresholds)). The State Pension sets the floor: **£241.30 a week (£12,547.60 a year) in 2026/27, rising to £250.70 a week (£13,036.40) in April 2027** ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/new-state-pension/what-youll-get); [MSE](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/news/2026/10/budget-state-pension-tax-free-allowance-rise/)). It needs 35 qualifying years for the full amount. State Pension age is rising from 66 to 67 between April 2026 and April 2028 ([Commons WPC](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5902/cmselect/cmworpen/76/report.html)). The triple lock stays until April 2030. After that, an "adjusted" lock raises the pension by the highest of CPI, 2.5%, or whatever is needed to keep its value against earnings over time ([GOV.UK factsheet](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/state-pension-uprating-analysis-2026/triple-lock-reform-factsheet)). Commentators describe the change inconsistently: Rathbones calls it a "double lock". Lessons should use the GOV.UK wording.

The FSCS figure needs fixing too: the familiar "£85,000 for deposits" is out of date for anything written after December 2025.

## The US course leads with the match and the 62-versus-70 choice

US federal sources make this the easiest course to build. They are public domain and figure-dense, and the two highest-value lessons need almost no interpretation. For 2026 the 401(k), 403(b), 457 and TSP deferral limit is **$24,500**. The catch-up for ages 50 and over is **$8,000**, and for ages 60–63 it is **$11,250**. The IRA limit is **$7,500** with a $1,100 catch-up ([IRS IR-2025-111](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500)). The employer match is the "free money" hook. The vesting schedule is what learners must check before changing jobs: employer money can vest on a cliff of up to 3 years or a graded schedule of 2–6 years ([DOL EBSA](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/publications/what-you-should-know-about-your-retirement-plan)). For Social Security, anyone born in 1960 or later has a full retirement age of 67 ([SSA](https://ssa.gov/international/Agreement_Pamphlets/full-retirement-age.html)). Claiming at 62 pays about **70%** of the full benefit, and waiting to 70 pays about **124%**, so a 70-year-old claimant gets roughly 77% more per month. SSA's own reduction page blocked automated fetching, so those percentages rest on [secondary confirmation](https://www.maximizemysocialsecurity.com/blog/social-security-full-retirement-age-by-birth-year). A credit takes $1,890 of earnings in 2026, 40 credits are needed, and the 2026 COLA is 2.8% ([SSA 2026 fact sheet](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html)).

The Saver's Match deserves a higher place than its slot suggests. The course audience is young and low-paid, and "save $2,000, get $1,000" is the best return in the US system. It ranks eighth only because Treasury's rulemaking (Notice 2026-48) is incomplete. The planned IRA directory carries the politically loaded name "TrumpIRA.gov", which lessons should describe neutrally. The readiness data gives the motivation: **only 35% of non-retired adults think their savings are on track, and only 7% of those earning under $25k** ([Fed SHED via InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/practice-management/fed-survey-reveals-cracks-beneath-stable-surface-of-us-financial-health/266580)). EBRI found the median retiree stopped work at **62**, and nearly half retired earlier than planned ([EBRI RCS 2026](https://www.ebri.org/content/2026-retirement-confidence-survey-finds-americans-less-confident-about-retirement-as-worries-grow-over-social-security--medicare-and-rising-costs)). The Social Security lesson should be framed as "reduced, not gone, unless Congress acts". That is accurate, and it avoids the doom framing that drives people to claim early.

## Four key sources forbid the reuse an AI tutor needs

The licence split decides how each lesson can be built. The tutor can be grounded in US federal and GOV.UK text. It can only cite and link the most-quoted private and regulator sources. South African material has to be rewritten from scratch.

## Give retirement its own course and trim the overlaps

Retirement and investing need their own course for three reasons. First, volume: the SA list alone runs to nine lessons, and its two core numbers sets (the cumulative lump-sum tables and the two-pot rules) cannot be compressed into money-lesson 7 without losing the "don't cash out" arithmetic. Second, update cadence: these facts change with February (SA), October and November (UK and US) budgets and annual adjustments, so they should sit in one file that the tutor reads with a "facts as at" date. Third, audience: a 19-year-old on a first payslip needs the money course's lesson 7 now, while the drawdown and annuity lessons serve a different learner.

The money course should keep lessons 7 and 8, but rewritten as gateways. Lesson 7, "Start early and keep it" (or "Retirement and tax-free savings"), should teach the early-bird example and one national "free money" fact: SA's TFSA and "don't cash out on resignation", the UK employer's 3%, the US match. Lesson 8, "Inflation and index funds", should teach global lessons 1, 5 and 6 compressed: inflation eats cash, a fee is charged on the whole balance every year, and most active funds lag the index. Both should end with a link into the full course.

The overlaps with the tax courses need clear owners, or the tutor will give two answers that drift apart. In SA, the existing six-lesson tax course does not cover retirement, but its proposed additions do. "Saving and investing tax" (interest exemption R23,800, TFSA, CGT exclusion R50,000) and "Two-pot withdrawals" both overlap here. The tax course should own the *mechanics*: tax directives, marginal-rate taxation, the auto-assessment around 21 July and the shortfall due by 20 October 2026, and the penalties for under-declaring ([SARS](https://www.sars.gov.za/media-release/more-than-2-million-taxpayers-withdraw-from-their-savings-pot/)). The retirement course should own the *decision*: whether to withdraw at all, preserving, and the lump-sum tables. Both should link to the same TFSA fact file rather than restate it. The RA deduction (R430,000 cap) belongs in the existing deductions-vs-credits tax lesson, with the retirement course linking to it.

In the UK, the planned tax course lists "ISAs" as lesson 6. Move ISAs to the retirement and investing course, which needs the full LISA and FTB ISA detail, and leave the tax course a one-paragraph pointer. Pension tax relief and the frozen £12,570 allowance interact: the new Pension Tax Allowance exists because the State Pension is about to cross the frozen threshold. So the UK tax course's "fiscal drag" lesson should link to retirement lesson 2. In the US, the tax course's credits lesson should mention the Saver's Credit and link to retirement lesson 8. The early-withdrawal 10% tax belongs to the retirement course, but its form-filing side (Form 5329, 1099-R) can stay with the tax course.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the problem from what to teach to what the tutor is allowed to know. The single most persuasive evidence for index investing, SPIVA, and the most-used US adviser check, BrokerCheck, are both off-limits to an AI tutor's retrieval index. The UK's best consumer guidance (MoneyHelper, FCA) can only be linked. The course's evidence layer therefore has to be hand-built: a versioned fact file of short, attributed statements. That is more work up front. It also turns out to be the same structure the update problem demands, because this year's changes (ISA reversal, LISA closure, Saver's Match, SDA doubling) arrived by budget announcement, not by any update the tutor would notice.

The South African course carries the most risk and the highest return. Its learners are the most likely to cash out (repeat two-pot withdrawals), the least protected by open-licensed material, and the most exposed to "10% a month" schemes. A single lesson showing that a R100,000 job-change cash-out costs R13,050 now and part of the R550,000 tax-free retirement amount later may do more good than the whole global course. Build that lesson first and source its edge cases (preservation funds, provident 55+ transition, legacy RAs) before anything else ships.
